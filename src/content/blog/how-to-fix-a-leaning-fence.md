## Why a fence leans: find the cause before you pick a fix

The right fix depends on the cause. Walk the whole run before you touch anything and note where the lean starts and stops.

- **Rot at the soil line.** Wood posts rot where they meet the ground. If a post swings at the base, or a screwdriver sinks into the wood at grade, that post is finished.
- **A loose or heaved footing.** If the post is sound but the concrete has cracked or shifted, the footing is the problem. Much of the Dallas area sits on expansive clay that swells and shrinks with moisture, which is a plausible factor in posts that drift over the years. It is a suspect, not something anyone can confirm about your yard from the street.
- **Storm wind.** The National Weather Service counts a North Texas thunderstorm with damaging winds of 58 mph or more as severe. A solid six-foot privacy fence catches wind like a sail, so one leaning post often means its neighbors took load too.
- **Water.** A sprinkler aimed at the boards, a downspout dumping at the base or a low spot that pools water softens the soil and speeds up rot.
- **Something pushing.** Tree roots, mulch banked against the pickets or a retaining wall that has moved can shove a fence over from one side.

One rotted post with sound neighbors is a repair. Several soft posts mean the fence is close to the end of its life, and the repair-versus-replace math changes. Our guide on [how long a wood fence lasts](/blog/how-long-does-a-wood-fence-last/) covers what to expect from wood in Texas conditions.

## How to Fix a Leaning Fence Post, Step by Step

This is for a single post with a loose footing, loose rails, or one post you have to replace. It is not a recipe for a whole fence line, and how well it works depends on your soil, your drainage and the age of the wood.

**Before you dig, call 811.** Texas811 says Texas law requires you or your contractor to contact 811 two business days before you dig, even in your own backyard, and that the locate service costs the homeowner nothing. If you hire a contractor, the notice is the contractor's job. See the [Texas811 homeowner page](https://texas811.org/homeowner/).

**Safety.** A leaning section can drop without warning, so keep your head and legs clear of the high side, skip gusty days and get a second person for any lifting. Keep the dog inside while the fence is open.

1. **Stop the lean.** Screw a diagonal 2x4 brace to the post and to a stake driven into the ground on the side the fence is leaning toward.
2. **Check whether the post is sound.** Probe the wood at ground level. Soft, dark or crumbling wood means the post has to be replaced, because no amount of re-plumbing fixes rot. Skip to step 5.
3. **Free a sound post.** Dig out the loose soil and any cracked concrete around the base until the post moves freely.
4. **Re-set it plumb.** Check it with a level on two adjacent faces, not one. Brace it, then backfill with concrete mix. Make the new footing wider than the old one and slope the top away from the post so rain runs off instead of pooling against the wood. Follow the bag's cure instructions before removing the braces or loading the panels.
5. **Replace a rotted post.** Cut the rails free, remove the post and as much of the old footing as you can, and set a new one. Pressure-treated wood rated for ground contact is the usual choice. Steel posts do not rot, but they cost more and an HOA may control fence type.
6. **Re-fasten the rails.** Use exterior-rated screws and metal brackets rather than nails. If the posts are fine, this alone may be the whole job.

Post-repair stakes and brackets can steady a sound post that has only leaned. They will not save a rotted one, and if clay movement caused the lean it can come back. Treat them as a stopgap.

## When a Leaning Fence Needs a Contractor, Not a Weekend

These jobs usually are not DIY:

- **More than a couple of posts have failed.** You are rebuilding a section, so compare the repair against a replacement.
- **The fence is taller than 6 feet.** The City of Dallas says a permit is required above 6 feet outside the front yard, and the maximum height is 9 feet.
- **A gate hangs off the leaning post.** A sagging gate that will not latch is a post problem first. If it has an opener, the power wiring is electrical work, so ask the installer who holds the electrical license. Our notes on [gate opener repair](/blog/gate-opener-repair/) explain what to check.
- **The fence is wrought iron, steel or masonry.** Welding and masonry footings are not weekend work.
- **A slope, retaining wall or drainage problem is involved.** Fix the water or the wall, or the fence resets the clock.

**An example, not a client.** Picture a 1972 ranch house in Lake Highlands with about 40 feet of cedar alley fence leaning toward the pavement. The pickets are fine, but three posts are soft at grade. A brace holds it overnight, yet replacing three posts means pulling old footings and matching rail height so the alley gate still lines up. That is arithmetic on one firm's range, not a quote. ## Permits, Neighbors and HOAs Before You Fix a Leaning Fence

Repair rules differ by suburb, so a repair that needs no paperwork in one city can need a permit in the next. These come from the cities' own pages. Check with the City and your HOA before you start.

- **Dallas.** The City's residential fence guide says no permit is needed for fences 4 feet or less in a front yard, or 6 feet or less elsewhere, except in areas such as historic, conservation and planned development districts. Applications ask you to list a general contractor.
- **Plano.** Repairs not exceeding 25 percent of the fence area in 12 months do not need a permit, per the city's handout.
- **Carrollton.** A permit is required to repair or relocate more than 20 feet of an existing fence. Replacing boards and posts up to 20 feet does not need one.

Texas does not license fence contractors at the state level, but some cities register them (Dallas, Richardson and Irving among them). Ask any contractor for city registration and proof of insurance. In many HOAs the association can still control fence type, so get written approval first.

**An example, not a neighbor of yours.** Say a Garland homeowner has a 60-foot shared back fence leaning toward their side. Who pays? Texas Law Help, a legal aid resource, says that where both neighbors use a fence straddling the boundary line, both own it and would share the cost of repairing and replacing it, while a fence wholly on one lot belongs to that owner. That is general information, not legal advice. Using the 6-foot cedar range one firm advertises ($18 to $28 per foot), 60 feet runs $1,080 to $1,680, and an equal split would be $540 to $840 each. The equal split is an assumption, so agree the scope and the split in writing before anyone books a crew.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I fix a leaning fence myself?

Sometimes. A sound post with a loose footing, or loose rails, is a reasonable job for two careful people with a level, braces and concrete mix, after an 811 call. A rotted post, a fence over 6 feet, a gate on a leaning post or a long run of failing posts is usually a contractor job.

### How much does it cost to fix a leaning fence in Dallas?

It depends on how many posts and how much fence. One DFW firm advertises $75 to $200 to reset a leaning post, a Dallas firm advertises $150 to $400 to pull a post and set a new footing, and storm repairs are advertised at $300 to $1,200 by one firm and $450 to $1,500 by another. Minimum service charges can apply, so ask for the total in writing.

### Do I need a permit to repair a leaning fence in Dallas?

The City of Dallas guide says fences 6 feet or less outside the front yard do not need a permit, except in areas such as historic, conservation and planned development districts. Fences over 6 feet do. Suburbs set their own repair thresholds, so check with your city and your HOA.

### Who pays if the leaning fence is shared with a neighbor?

Texas Law Help says that if both neighbors use a fence straddling the boundary line, both own it and would share the cost of repair and replacement. A fence wholly on one lot belongs to that owner. That is general information, not legal advice, so talk to your neighbor first and put the split in writing.
