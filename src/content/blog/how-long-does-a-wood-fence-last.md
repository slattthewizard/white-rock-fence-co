## How Long Does a Wood Fence Last in Dallas? The Honest Answer

There is no single number, and anyone who gives you one without looking at your fence is guessing. Dallas-area fence contractors publish prices freely, but none of the pages we read gave a lifespan figure we could trace to a reliable source, so this article does not invent one.

What we can say is what separates a fence that gets tired early from one that keeps going. Wood type matters, but how well the posts are set matters at least as much. So do drainage, sun, sprinklers, wind exposure and whether anyone has sealed the wood since it went up.

Think of a wood fence as two parts with different lives. The pickets and rails are cheap to swap one at a time. The posts hold everything up, and when they fail, patching the boards on top of them buys very little.

## What Shortens a Wood Fence's Life in North Texas

**Clay that moves.** Much of the Dallas area sits on expansive clay that swells and shrinks with moisture. A TxDOT research report from UT Arlington and the Texas Transportation Institute describes active soil depths of 10 to 20 feet in the Dallas-Fort Worth area, although it was written about pavement and says nothing about fence posts. You can read it as [TxDOT research report 0-5179-1](https://static.tti.tamu.edu/tti.tamu.edu/documents/0-5179-1.pdf). The takeaway is modest: ground that heaves and shrinks gives posts a harder job, so ask every bidder how they set them. We are not quoting a post depth, because the sources we found disagree.

**Moisture at the soil line.** Rot tends to start where wood meets wet ground, and in boards that never fully dry out after the sprinkler hits them. A fence whose pickets look fine but whose posts rock in your hand is closer to the end than one with ugly grey boards on solid posts.

**Wind.** The National Weather Service office in Fort Worth classes a thunderstorm with damaging winds of 58 mph or more as severe, and North Texas gets them. Its handout does not say what wind speed takes down a fence, so neither do we. A tall solid fence catches more wind than a lower open one, which is worth remembering if you are thinking about going to 8 feet.

**Sun and no upkeep.** Grey, cracked boards are what sun does to unsealed wood. Weathering alone does not mean the fence has failed, but cracked pickets let water in at the nail holes and board ends, which can speed up decay.

## Pine or Cedar: Does the Wood Change How Long a Fence Lasts?

Cedar costs more up front. One DFW firm advertises that cedar typically runs 20 to 35 percent more for materials, and the same firm lists a 150-foot pressure-treated pine fence at $2,200 to $3,300 installed against $3,000 to $4,500 for western red cedar. That is one contractor's range, not a Dallas average.

Whether the extra money buys more years is a fair question and an unsettled one in our sources. Cedar is often described as naturally more rot-resistant than untreated pine, and pressure-treated pine is treated for ground contact. Beyond that, a pine fence with well-set posts and regular staining can outlast a cedar fence with poorly set posts and no upkeep. The build and the maintenance tend to matter as much as the species.

Here is an example scenario, not a real project. A Richardson owner is comparing pine and cedar for an 8-foot board-on-board fence along the back lot line. Richardson's own page limits fences to a maximum of 8 feet from the rear property line to the front wall of the house and requires a permit for any fence 2 1/2 feet or taller. The city also says fence contractors must be registered to work there.

On price, we could not source a Dallas firm's per-foot figure for an 8-foot fence. A national cost guide puts a 150-foot fence at $2,250 to $6,000 for 6 feet and $4,500 to $12,750 for 8 feet, so height is expensive. Board-on-board adds roughly $2 to $5 per linear foot over a basic dog-ear fence according to one DFW firm, and about 30 percent according to another.

The owner's best move is to ask each bidder, in writing, what posts they use, how they set them, and what stain schedule they recommend. Steel posts are one option worth asking about, and the contractor will quote the difference.

## Wood Fence Lifespan in Practice: Repair or Replace?

**Repair makes sense when** the damage is local and the posts are sound. A few missing pickets, a sagging gate, one leaning post with the rest solid, or a single storm-damaged run are all repair jobs.

**Replacement deserves a quote when** many posts are soft at the soil line, sections keep leaning after being straightened, or the repair bids for one stretch start to look like a fraction of a new fence. Ask for both numbers on the same sheet of paper.

Here is another example scenario. A 1972 ranch house in Lake Highlands has a leaning alley fence along its back line. The pickets are grey, three posts move when pushed, and the rest are firm. One Dallas firm advertises pulling a post and pouring a new footing at $150 to $400, and straightening a leaning 10 to 30 foot section at $300 to $800. At that range, two leaning sections come to $600 to $1,600.

If the rest of the run is sound, fixing those sections and restaining may be the sensible call. If the other posts are also soft at the soil line, the same money only postpones a bigger bill, and the owner should ask for a replacement quote beside the repair quote. An alley fence also has a City rule to respect: Dallas keeps the zone between 2 1/2 and 8 feet high clear inside a visibility triangle at an alley or driveway edge.

A third example scenario shows the other way fences end. A Plano family has a section down after a May storm, with a dog in the yard. They need a barrier quickly, and they need to know if the neighboring sections will follow.

The honest first step is to ask a contractor to look at the posts on either side of the gap before quoting a patch. Storms are often the day an aging fence finally fails, but a sound fence can lose a section too, and the fix differs. One Dallas firm advertises a full panel rebuild after wind at $1,200 to $3,500 for 20 to 50 feet. Another DFW firm advertises storm damage repair at $300 to $1,200.

## Stretching a Fence's Life: Staining, Posts and Paperwork

**Staining.** One DFW-area firm advertises staining and sealing every 2 to 3 years at $150 to $500 per treatment, plus $75 to $200 for pressure washing. Another DFW firm advertises $1.00 to $2.50 per linear foot, or $150 to $375 for a 150-foot fence. Staining slows weathering. It does not fix a rotting post or straighten a leaning one.

**Posts.** When a post does fail, replacing it is a digging job. Texas law requires you or your contractor to contact 811 two business days before you dig, and [Texas811](https://texas811.org/homeowner/) says that when you hire a contractor, calling is the contractor's responsibility. Confirm it before anyone breaks ground.

**Paperwork on a replacement.** Piecemeal repair has a ceiling. Plano's handout says a permit is not required for repairs to existing fences that do not exceed 25 percent of the fence area in a 12-month period. Richardson requires a permit when replacing 25 percent or more of a fence. Mesquite's code sets the line at replacing 50 percent or more, and Carrollton's city page at repairing more than 20 feet in length. Each city is different, so confirm with your own.

In Dallas, the City's fence permit checklist says a permit is required for fences over 4 feet in a front yard or over 6 feet elsewhere, with a maximum height of 9 feet. The City's guide says fences of 6 feet or less in other locations need no permit, except in areas with extra rules such as historic, conservation and planned development districts. It also says applications require a general contractor to be added as the contractor.

Texas has no state licence category for fence contractors, though some cities register them, including Dallas, Richardson and Irving. Ask for city registration and proof of insurance, and confirm requirements with the City at (214) 948-4480.

**HOA.** In many Texas HOAs the association can still control the type of fencing under Property Code section 202.023, so get written approval before the work starts.

**A shared fence.** If you and a neighbor both use a fence that straddles the line, TexasLawHelp says both of you own it and would share the cost of repairing or replacing it. A fence wholly on one lot belongs to that owner. That is general information, not legal advice, and a conversation with the neighbor before the quotes arrive saves an argument afterward.

Whichever way your fence is heading, ask for two or three written quotes from independent contractors, each pricing the repair and the replacement, and compare them line by line for post material, how posts are set, gate hardware and cleanup.
